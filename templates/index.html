The monitor

Every outing, against his own normal

A pitcher's average fastball moves from outing to outing for boring reasons: he threw 4 fastballs or 40, it was cold, he'd had nine days off. A 1-mph dip can be noise for one pitcher and alarming for another, so each pitcher gets his own model.

A state-space filter. For every pitcher and all five vital signs, a Kalman filter tracks his true level. It expects each outing's average to wander by his own pitch-to-pitch noise divided by the fastballs thrown (about {{sd_velo}} mph per pitch for a typical arm), plus day-to-day noise. The longer he's been away, the more it lets his level drift, so rest, injured-list stints and the off-season don't set off false alarms.

Hierarchical. Each pitcher's noise is shrunk toward the league's. The drift and day-to-day noise are learned from every pitcher at once by maximum likelihood, separately for starters and relievers.

Calibrated. Each outing gets a score: how surprising it was given everything before it, in standard deviations. On {{calib_n}} healthy outings the scores are standard normal (SD {{calib_sd}}; {{calib_tail}} beyond ±2), so −3 means the same thing for a starter who threw 60 fastballs and a reliever who threw 6.

Can it see an injury coming?

No early warning. Then a drop.

A common belief is that velocity slips for weeks before an arm injury. I lined up the monitor's scores for the six outings before each of {{arm_events}} arm injured-list stints (elbow, forearm, shoulder, lat) and compared them with {{non_events}} non-arm stints (oblique, hamstring and so on) as a placebo.

Before the final outing, the arm group's average score stays within ±{{arm_early_max}} SD of normal, about a tenth of a mph at most. Across those five outings it differs from the placebo by {{es_pre_diff}} SD (95% CI {{es_pre_lo}} to {{es_pre_hi}}), so there's no detectable warning. On the final outing, the arm group's fastball score falls to {{arm_final_velo}} SD and its spin score to {{arm_final_spin}} SD, against {{non_final_velo}} SD before non-arm injuries: a gap of {{es_final_diff}} SD ({{es_final_lo}} to {{es_final_hi}}). The injury shows up the day it happens, not weeks before.

Could a model find a warning I couldn't see? I trained one on {{w_train_season}} (gradient boosting plus logistic regression, 20 features: every signal, drift accumulators, workload, age, injury history) and tested it on every outing of {{w_test_season}}. It reached an AUC of {{w_auc}}, barely better than a coin flip and no better than a one-line rule (fastball 1.5 mph under his season average: {{w_auc_rule}}). Workload alone got {{w_auc_work}}. Flagging the riskiest 2% of outings caught {{w_early}} of the {{w_events}} arm stints before their final outing. Public tracking data doesn't carry a weeks-ahead warning. If a model built on it claims one, check it for leakage first.

In the game

Catching it before he walks off

If the injury shows up the day it happens, the question becomes whether the dugout can see it while he's still pitching. For every fastball, the alert compares velocity with what this pitcher should throw at that point: his filtered level from previous outings, plus the league's normal fatigue curve. A CUSUM adds up shortfalls that repeat, shrugs off a single slow pitch, and fires when the total crosses a threshold.

Tuned to fire on 5% of ordinary outings, it fired during {{ig_all_caught}} of all {{ig_all_n}} outings that ended in an arm injured-list stint ({{ig_all_lift}}× chance), a median of {{ig_left}} fastballs before the pitcher left. When the placement was effective the next day, the likeliest sign the injury happened in that game, it flagged {{ig_nd_caught}} of {{ig_nd_n}}. When it came two or more days later, {{ig_later_caught}} of {{ig_later_n}}. It flagged {{ig_sprain_hits}} of the {{ig_sprain_n}} sprains, which tend to end an outing at once.

For a staff, that setting means about {{ig_team_alerts}} alerts a season on ordinary outings, roughly one every {{ig_games_per_alert}} games, and it would have caught about {{ig_team_caught}} of the {{ig_team_injuries}} arm-injury outings a typical team has in a season. It can't see a single-pitch tear coming. It's a prompt for a trainer's look or a mound visit, not a reason to pull anyone.

Does rest help?

A natural experiment from the schedule

Comparing starters on five days' rest with starters on six is confounded: managers give extra days to the pitchers who need them. Team off days are different. They're fixed when the schedule is written, months ahead, and rainouts come from the weather. When one falls in the five days after a start ({{rest_offshare}} of the {{rest_starts}} turns studied), his next turn gets pushed back for reasons that have nothing to do with his arm.

Using off days as an instrument for days of rest (two-stage least squares, pitcher-season fixed effects, month and previous-pitch-count controls, errors clustered by pitcher), each off day adds {{rest_fs}} days of rest (first-stage F = {{rest_F}}). Each extra day changed next-start fastball velocity by {{rest_velo}} mph (95% CI {{rest_velo_lo}} to {{rest_velo_hi}}) and spin by {{rest_spin}} rpm ({{rest_spin_lo}} to {{rest_spin_hi}}).

In other words, within a normal rotation, extra rest doesn't buy velocity. All {{rest_versions}} versions of the experiment come out slightly negative ({{rest_range_lo}} to {{rest_range_hi}} mph per day), and none leaves room for a gain of more than {{rest_gain_cap}} mph per day. A six-man rotation may have other benefits, such as fewer innings per arm over a season, which this design doesn't measure. A harder fastball the next time out isn't one of them.

Method, validation and limits

How it was built, and how it was checked

  1. Data. Every {{seasons}} regular-season pitch from Baseball Savant ({{pitches}} pitches), loaded into DuckDB and aggregated to one row per outing for each pitcher's primary fastball. Position players who pitched are left out. {{il_total}} pitcher injured-list placements parsed from MLB's transaction log, dated to their retroactive start and grouped by body part ({{il_arm}} elbow, forearm, shoulder or lat). Contusions and lacerations, which come from being hit rather than from throwing, are kept out of the arm group.
  2. Monitor. A local-level state-space model per pitcher and metric. Observation noise is his within-game variance (partially pooled) divided by the fastballs thrown, plus a day-level term; drift grows with days away. Hyperparameters are fit by maximum marginal likelihood across all pitchers, by role. A pitcher's first outing sets his baseline; every outing after it is scored.
  3. Event study. Arm injuries vs non-arm injuries as a placebo, aligned on the final outing and compared stint by stint: the average of the five earlier outings, and the final one.
  4. Early warning. Gradient boosting and logistic regression (and their average) on 20 outing-level features, trained on {{w_train_season}} and tested on {{w_test_season}}, against three baselines. Event-level results separate a warning on the final outing from one earlier.
  5. In-game alert. A one-sided CUSUM on pitch-level velocity scores, with expectations from the filter and the league fatigue curve by role. Thresholds are set on ordinary outings only, counting every outing with a fastball, however short. They hold across seasons: {{ig_ord_rates}} of ordinary outings alert in 2025 and 2026.
  6. Rest. Two-stage least squares. The instrument is the number of team off days (scheduled or rained out) in the five days after the previous start, a fixed window so the manager's choice can't leak in. His previous appearance must be a full start, so a relief outing or short start in between can't blur the rest count, and turns near the All-Star break are dropped. Pitcher-season fixed effects, month and previous-pitch-count controls, errors clustered by pitcher. Robust to counting off days over the whole gap and to a tighter rest window.

Note 1What didn't work

I also tested whether the filter confirms a real velocity drop faster than simple rules, by planting known drops in real pitchers' sequences. At the same false-alarm rate it was no faster for starters: it confirmed a 1.5-mph drop within three starts {{chg_filter}} of the time, against {{chg_single}} for a plain "down vs his season average" rule. For relievers it was a little faster ({{chg_rp_filter}} vs {{chg_rp_single}}). Its value is calibration, meaning a score that means the same thing for every pitcher and sample size, not speed. The early-warning model is the other thing that didn't work.

Note 2Limits

Public data has no medical records, biomechanics or bullpen workloads, which is where a team's own model starts. Injured-list dates are when a team placed a player, not when an injury began, and some arm injuries are listed vaguely. The in-game alert can't see a pitcher who was pulled early because a trainer already noticed. It reads only the primary fastball; scoring every pitch type against its own expected speed is the obvious next step.
